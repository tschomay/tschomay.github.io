title: "Auralux: the best UI decision was deleting a button"
---

<p align="justify">
I've been building <b>Auralux</b> — a full-screen audio-reactive visualizer meant for a phone propped on a charger stand, turning whatever's playing in the room into light. It's grown into forty-one visualizers across seven switchable packs — a run through the '60s liquid light show, '70s mirror balls, '80s VHS tracking artifacts, '90s screensavers, plus a Nature pack and a deliberately quiet Ambient one — every setting tunable on the device, installable straight to the home screen. Not a work project. Just something I wanted to exist, built mostly in conversation with Claude Code.
</p>

<p align="justify">
The most interesting decision in the whole project was deleting a button.
</p>

<p align="justify">
Early on, Auralux had a Start button, because that's what you build first. But the target scenario is a phone on a stand — nobody wants to unlock it and tap something just to see the room's music turn into color. So I made it auto-start on load instead. And that's when the button became a problem: once the engine starts itself, a Start button can only ever be drawn disabled and relabeled "Running" — a control sitting in the most prominent spot on the screen with nothing left to do. I tried it that way, looked at it, and pulled it out entirely.
</p>

<p align="justify">
But the button's one real job — telling you something went wrong and letting you retry — still needed a home. That folded into a status line that stays invisible until the audio path actually has something to report: a screen share with no audio track, a capture killed from the browser's own sharing bar, a silent fallback to the microphone. It turns red, says what happened, and gives you a Retry that repeats the exact thing that failed. Before that, every audio failure was a bare console log — which, on a phone sitting across the room, is indistinguishable from no error handling at all.
</p>

<p align="justify">
A smaller version of the same instinct shows up in how the app updates itself. The obvious move for an installable web app is a cache-first service worker — it's the standard advice, and it's wrong for this specific case. A phone on a stand sits on one network for days at a time; the failure mode that actually matters isn't "offline in a tunnel," it's "silently pinned to a build from last week." So the service worker is network-first instead, and precaches nothing — every deploy reaches the device on its next natural load rather than requiring anyone to notice it's stale.
</p>

<p align="justify">
Neither of those was a hard engineering problem. They were both just refusals to ship the default answer once I'd actually pictured the phone sitting there, unattended, for real. That's the part of building things I still enjoy most, technical or not: the interesting decision is rarely the one with the obvious right answer — it's the one where the textbook default and the actual use case quietly disagree.
</p>
